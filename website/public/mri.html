What you are looking at

Radiologists rarely rely on one sequence. They compare several, because each answers a different question: what is the anatomy, where is the water, which tissue is abnormal, and what lights up after contrast.

The lesion in this illustration is invented. Its outline in amber is what a radiologist might mark as a region of interest.

How MRI works, briefly

A strong magnet lines up hydrogen atoms in the body's water. Radio pulses knock them out of line, and the scanner listens as they relax back. Different tissues relax at different speeds, and that difference becomes contrast.

MRI uses no ionizing radiation, unlike CT. It is excellent for soft tissue such as the brain, which is why it is the preferred scan for brain tumors. Scans take roughly 20 to 60 minutes, are loud, and require lying still inside a tube. People with some metal implants or devices cannot be scanned safely, so staff ask detailed screening questions.

Three ways to cut a brain

  • Axial slices run from front to back like looking down from above. They are the most common view, and the one this project uses.
  • Coronal slices look from the front, showing left and right and the base of the brain. They are especially useful for pituitary tumors.
  • Sagittal slices look from the side and show the midline structures in profile.

What radiologists look for

  • Location. Inside the brain, on its surface or at the skull base narrows the possibilities.
  • Borders. Sharp edges suggest a tumor that pushes tissue aside, and blurry edges suggest one that infiltrates it.
  • Enhancement. Whether, how much and in what pattern the lesion brightens after contrast.
  • Mass effect. Squashed ventricles, shifted midline or swelling that shows the lesion is pushing on the rest of the brain.
  • Change over time. Comparing with earlier scans is often the most telling clue.

Notice how much of this depends on seeing the whole volume and comparing sequences. A model that sees one slice of one sequence is working with far less than a radiologist, which is a main reason its accuracy has a ceiling. More on the model.
