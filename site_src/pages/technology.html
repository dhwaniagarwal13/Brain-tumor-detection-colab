How the classifier works

A convolutional neural network turns an image into four probabilities. Here is each step, and what could go wrong at it.

  1. Prepare the image

    The slice is converted to three channels, resized (224 x 224 for the current model, 128 x 128 for the earlier VGG16 baseline) and scaled so pixel values sit in a small range. Training uses exactly the same steps as prediction, otherwise results drift.

  2. Find features with convolutions

    Small filters slide across the image and respond to edges, textures and shapes. Early layers find simple patterns, and deeper layers combine them into things like blobs, borders and bright rings.

  3. Shrink and summarise

    Pooling layers keep the strongest responses and discard detail, so the network cares about what is present more than exactly where.

  4. Decide with a small head

    A short stack of dense layers turns the features into four raw scores, one per class. Dropout randomly switches off units during training so the model cannot lean on any single one.

  5. Turn scores into probabilities

    A function called softmax converts the four scores into numbers that are positive and add up to 100%. The largest becomes the predicted class.

Transfer learning: borrowing eyes

With about 900 training images, a network learning from scratch would memorise them. Instead both models start from a network already trained on millions of everyday photos.

Edges, textures and shapes are useful in any image, brain scans included. We keep those learned filters, add a new small head for the four tumor classes, and then train gently. Fine-tuning goes one step further and lets the top few layers of the borrowed network adjust to MRI as well.

Confidence is not correctness

A softmax probability says how the model's scores compare with one another, not how often it is right. Networks trained to minimise error are often over-confident, so a "92%" can be wrong far more than 8% of the time. Smoothing the labels during training, as the newer model does, helps keep scores honest. Even so, this site shows confidence as a hint about uncertainty and never as a guarantee.

The model has seen only four categories. Show it a scan with a different problem, such as a stroke, or a different body part, and it will still pick one of the four. It has no "none of the above" option.
